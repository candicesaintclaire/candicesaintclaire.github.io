c. 1849
Historical Chart

Stream of Time

Charles Smith & Sons, 172 The Strand, London

Acquired from the estate of a University of Washington archivist, folded to roughly 7×9 inches — its scale entirely unknown until carefully unfolded at home to reveal a four-foot chronological chart of world history. The decision not to open it in the storage unit, to wait, to bring it somewhere safe first: that instinct preceded the knowledge of what it was.

Documented provenance traces ownership across 24 years: inscribed to J.B. Bartlett in 1860, passed to M.J.L. Bartlett in 1884. Research into the publisher, the edition, and comparable holdings led to correspondence with four major institutions. Current evidence suggests this may be one of only two known surviving copies.

Institutional Verification
Correspondence confirmed with the Library of Congress, the British Library, the National Library of Scotland, and Stanford University's David Rumsey Map Center.
1826
Rare Books

Shakespeare Complete Works

Chiswick Press — with hidden fore-edge paintings

I owned this set for six months before I saw what was on it. I didn't know what fore-edge paintings were when I first acquired them. The discovery came gradually — a certain angle of light, a fanned page — and then all at once.

Fore-edge paintings are a rare decorative technique in which artwork is applied to the edges of a book's pages, visible only when the pages are fanned at a specific angle and otherwise completely concealed. Their presence on a volume of this age and provenance is significant. This find is less a story of expertise than of patience — of not rushing, of staying curious, of looking again.

1704
Antiquarian Book

History of Lapland

Original 1704 edition

This one taught me more than any find before or since — not because of what it was, but because of the mistakes I made researching it. My initial approach to WorldCat and OCLC was imprecise. I pulled incorrect edition data. I made assumptions I shouldn't have made.

Working through those errors carefully, correcting them, and understanding why they happened shaped the research methodology I use today. Every step is now documented. Every claim is now traceable. Nothing is assumed. I include this here not despite the mistakes, but because of them.

1920s–1950s
Limited Edition Folios

French Limited Edition Folios

Paris — Numbered editions of 450 and 600

Two sets of Parisian folios — one documenting the contents of an old collector's home, one cataloging French costume and furniture — acquired together, each a numbered limited edition. The volumes are cream-colored, fine in a way that reminds me of lace. One set comes with loose illustration plates in a ribbon-tied case; the ribbons, miraculously, are intact.

One volume has significant spine separation. Before any outreach to French auction houses, the rebinding comes first. Some things take as long as they take. That's not a delay — that's stewardship.

Tools & Projects

Built when the existing tools weren't enough.

Token Context Counter
Python · AI Research Tooling
GitHub

A tokenizer built specifically for managing AI context windows during archival research sessions. When working with large language models on complex provenance research, knowing your exact token count isn't optional — it's the difference between a useful session and a truncated one. Built from a real workflow problem.

Roman Numeral Counter
Python · Research Tooling
GitHub

Built to solve a specific problem: vintage sheet music dating relies heavily on reading Roman numerals accurately and quickly. This tool supports that research by handling numeral conversion and counting at scale — turning a tedious manual step into a reliable automated one.
